Nightly, 3x Week or Weekly: Choosing a Frequency

How to match your cleaning schedule to your facility's actual soil load and occupancy pattern — not a default.

Resource

Cleaning frequency is the most consequential decision in a commercial cleaning contract, and it is almost always made by defaulting to whatever the last contractor provided. The result is either over-cleaning (paying for nightly service in a building that's occupied two days a week) or under-cleaning (running weekly service in a medical building that sees 80 patients a day).

This guide covers how to choose the right frequency for your Saint Paul commercial facility, based on the factors that actually determine soil load.

The Right Question Is Soil Load, Not Building Size

Cleaning frequency should match the rate at which your facility accumulates soil and biological contamination — not its square footage. A 20,000 sq ft warehouse with five employees needs less frequent cleaning than a 2,000 sq ft dental suite that sees 30 patients a day. Square footage is an input to pricing, not to frequency.

The primary factors that determine appropriate soil load in a Saint Paul commercial building:

  • Daily occupancy. How many people are in the building on a typical day? A building with 5 occupants accumulates soil more slowly than one with 50.
  • External traffic. Does the public visit your facility? Client-facing businesses — retail, medical, professional services with walk-in appointments — accumulate soil faster than back-office operations with no external visitors.
  • Floor type. Carpet retains particulate soil between cleanings; hard floors show it immediately. A heavily carpeted office may show less visible dirt than a hard-floor-only building at the same soil rate — but it's accumulating the same load in the fibers.
  • Activities in the space. Food preparation, patient care, light manufacturing, and retail all generate different types and rates of soil.
  • Season. In Minnesota, winter salt tracking increases soil load significantly from November through March. A building that holds with 3x/week service in summer may need nightly service during salt season.

Nightly Service (5x/Week): When It's Right

Nightly service is appropriate for any facility that has significant daily external traffic, produces biological waste (medical, food service), has occupancy patterns that leave residual soil each day, or is managed to a high appearance standard with no tolerance for overnight soil accumulation. In the Saint Paul commercial market, this includes most professional services offices with client-facing operations, all medical and dental facilities, retail storefronts, and multi-tenant buildings with shared lobbies and restrooms.

Three-Day Service (3x/Week): When It's Right

Three-day service works well for facilities where occupancy is concentrated on certain days, where the building is primarily back-office with no external visitors, or where the budget constraint is real but nightly service isn't necessary for appearance standards. In Saint Paul, this is common for small professional offices (under 2,000 sq ft) with low daily occupancy and light-use restrooms. A Monday-Wednesday-Friday schedule is standard; it ensures the facility is cleaned going into the weekend, after the midweek accumulation, and at the start of the week.

One caution: a three-day schedule on a building that actually needs nightly service produces a visible deterioration in appearance standards by the third day between cleans. If the building ever looks dirty by the second day without a clean, the schedule needs to increase.

Weekly Service: When It's Right

Weekly cleaning is appropriate for facilities with very low occupancy, used primarily for storage or infrequent meetings, or with minimal restroom use. In the Saint Paul commercial market, this is a minority of accounts. It is not appropriate for any building with daily occupancy above a handful of people or with client-facing operations.

Minnesota's Salt Season: The Seasonal Adjustment

The standard frequency recommendation above applies to the eight months of the year without significant road salt. From approximately November 15 through March 31 in a typical Twin Cities winter, tracked-in calcium chloride and sodium chloride increase the soil load on entry vestibules, lobbies, and corridor floors significantly. Buildings that run 3x/week in the off-season often need 5x/week in winter. Buildings that already run nightly may need additional mat service or an interim scrub added to the schedule.

We plan for this in our Saint Paul cleaning contracts — salt season adjustments are part of the scope discussion at walkthrough, not a surcharge you discover in January.

Start with a trial frequency if you're unsure. Begin at the recommended frequency, run it for 30 days, and evaluate whether the building looks appropriately maintained at the end of each clean cycle. If it looks clean just before the next scheduled clean, the frequency is probably right. If it looks dirty after day two without a clean, increase the frequency.
