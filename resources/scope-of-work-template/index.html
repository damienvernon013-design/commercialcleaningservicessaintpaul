What Belongs in a Commercial Cleaning Scope of Work

The document that prevents 90% of contractor disputes — and what most cleaning companies leave out of it.

Resource

A commercial cleaning scope of work is the document that defines, in writing, exactly what the cleaning contractor will do and how often. In a properly run cleaning contract, the scope is the reference document for both parties — the crew uses it as a checklist, and the facility manager uses it as the standard against which service quality is measured.

Most cleaning disputes in Saint Paul commercial buildings stem from the absence of this document, or from a scope so vague it provides no practical guidance. This article covers what a complete scope looks like.

Room-by-Room Task Assignment

The most important structural feature of a useful scope is room-level specificity. A scope that says "clean all offices" does not tell the crew which offices, whether private offices have the same tasks as open areas, or whether the conference room is treated differently from a standard workstation area.

A room-by-room format assigns tasks to each named or numbered space in the building. For a typical 8,000 sq ft Saint Paul professional building, this means separate sections for: lobby, each private office, open areas, each conference room, each restroom, the kitchen or break room, copy or storage rooms, corridors, stairwells, and any specialty areas.

Task Description and Frequency

Each task in the scope needs a description specific enough to be verifiable, and a frequency. Verifiable means a manager walking through the space the morning after a clean can determine whether it was done. Frequency is stated as "nightly," "weekly," "monthly," or "annually" — not "as needed," which is unenforceable.

Examples of scope language that works:

  • "Empty and reline all waste receptacles — nightly"
  • "Disinfect all toilet and urinal fixtures, inside and out — nightly"
  • "High-speed buff main corridor VCT — monthly"
  • "Extract all carpet — annually, before June 1"

Examples that don't work:

  • "General cleaning as needed"
  • "Clean restrooms thoroughly"
  • "Maintain floors in good condition"

What Crew Members Are Responsible For

In a multi-crew operation, the scope should indicate which crew or crew role is responsible for each section of the building. In a single-crew building, this is implicit — but if the contractor uses a rotating crew, it matters. Who is responsible for locking up? Who checks the alarm? Who is the first call if a restroom runs out of supplies mid-week?

What Is Explicitly Excluded

The exclusions section of a scope prevents scope creep in both directions. If tenants ask the cleaning crew to move furniture, handle equipment, or perform tasks not in the scope, the scope document gives the crew a basis to say no — and the facility manager a basis to address it with tenants.

Common exclusions to list explicitly: furniture moving, window washing (exterior), pressure washing, carpet repair, light bulb replacement, and anything touching locked personal items or secured areas.

Supply and Equipment Responsibility

The scope should state who provides cleaning supplies and equipment. In most commercial cleaning contracts in Saint Paul, the contractor provides both. If the facility provides specific consumables — soap, paper towels, toilet paper — that should be stated, along with who is responsible for restocking notification.

Access and Security Protocol

The scope document should include, or reference a separate document covering: entry codes, key fob procedures, alarm codes and arm/disarm sequence, which areas the crew may not enter, vehicle parking during service hours, and the procedure if the crew finds a security issue (door unlocked, alarm triggered).

The 30-day review: The scope should include a provision for a 30-day review call between the facility manager and the contractor. The purpose is to identify tasks that need to be added or adjusted based on actual use — things that a walkthrough doesn't always reveal.
